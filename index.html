Case Study 01 · Customer Analytics

RFM Customer Segmentation — from 4,372 anonymous customers to 6 clear action groups.

Discuss this project

A year of e-commerce transactions across 38 countries, £8.9M in revenue, and no way to tell which customers were worth protecting. I built an RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) model in SQL and Python, scored every customer, and packaged the result into a Power BI dashboard segmenting the base into six actionable groups.

4,372Total Customers
22,190Total Orders
£8.9MTotal Revenue
£401.5Avg Order Value
01 / Problem

Every customer looked the same on a revenue report.

The business could see total revenue but had no way to tell a loyal repeat buyer from a one-time purchaser about to churn — so every marketing spend treated the whole base the same way.

02 / Approach

Score every customer on Recency, Frequency and Monetary value.

Cleaned and deduplicated 22,190 orders in SQL, computed R/F/M scores per customer in Python, then mapped every combination to six segments and built a Power BI dashboard with revenue, distribution and density views.

Raw order history→ SQL cleaning & deduplication→ Python RFM scoring→ Power BI segmentation dashboard
03 / Findings

18% of customers drive over a third of all revenue.

  • Champions (18% of customers, 787 people) generate £3.16M — the single largest revenue segment on their own.
  • Champions + Loyal Customers together are 42% of the base but contribute 61.2% of total revenue.
  • At Risk and Lost customers combined are 21% of the base (920 customers) representing £1.2M in revenue exposed to churn.
  • The average RFM score sits at 9.7 out of 15 — the base is skewed toward mid-value, meaning targeted upgrades have real headroom.
04 / Recommendation

Protect the top, convert the middle, win back the edge.

Prioritize retention offers for Champions and Loyal Customers (61.2% of revenue), run conversion campaigns for Potential Loyalists, and launch a targeted re-engagement sequence for At Risk and Lost segments before that £1.2M churns out permanently.

Case Study 02 · HR Analytics

Employee Attrition Dashboard — turning a 15% churn number into six factors HR can act on.

↗ View on GitHub Discuss this project

A 5,000-employee HR dataset with a flat 15% attrition rate and no clear driver behind it. I cleaned and modeled the data in SQL and Python, then built a Power BI dashboard that breaks attrition down by department, overtime, income, commute distance and job role.

5,000Employees Analyzed
15.0%Overall Attrition Rate
29.3%Attrition — Overtime Staff
3.4xOvertime Risk Multiplier
01 / Problem

750 people left. Nobody could say why.

Leadership had a single churn number but no breakdown — no way to tell if attrition was a pay problem, a workload problem, or concentrated in one department. Every retention initiative so far was a guess.

02 / Approach

SQL for ground truth, Python for the patterns, Power BI for the story.

Cleaned and validated the raw HR export in SQL, engineered features in Python (income quartiles, commute-distance bands, overtime flag), then built five linked visuals in Power BI — all filtering against one KPI header.

Raw HR export→ SQL cleaning & validation→ Python feature engineering→ Power BI dashboard
03 / Findings

Three risk factors stood out — "department" wasn't one of them.

  • Overtime is the single biggest driver: 29.3% attrition among overtime staff vs. 8.7% for those who don't — a 3.4x gap.
  • Attrition tracks commute distance non-linearly: it climbs from 12.5% (0–10km) to a peak of 27.0% (31–40km), then eases to 22.1%.
  • Pay matters most at the bottom: the lowest income quartile churns at 24.2% vs. 8.2% in the top quartile — a 3x gap a flat raise wouldn't close efficiently.
  • Role risk is inverted from expectation: Executives (21.8%) and Analysts (17.1%) show higher attrition than Managers (4.7%) — the usual "promote them" retention lever doesn't apply here.
04 / Recommendation

Three targeted interventions instead of one blanket policy.

Cap sustained overtime in the highest-risk departments, revisit compensation specifically for the bottom income quartile, and build a dedicated retention track for Executive/Analyst roles rather than assuming the standard management-ladder incentive applies to everyone.
